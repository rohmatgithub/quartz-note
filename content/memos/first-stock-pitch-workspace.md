# First Stock Pitch Workspace

This first practice memo uses Coca-Cola because the business is understandable, globally recognized, cash generative, and easier to analyze than a highly complex technology or financial company.

## Candidate Company

- Company: The Coca-Cola Company
- Ticker: KO
- Sector: Consumer staples / beverages
- Why this company is a good first pitch: simple product economics, strong brand power, long operating history, and a business model that is easier to explain than most companies

## 1. Business Overview

- Coca-Cola owns beverage brands and earns economics from concentrates, syrups, and brand-led distribution relationships
- The business is driven by a portfolio of global beverage brands rather than one single product
- The most important feature of the model is that brand strength and distribution scale can support durable pricing power
- This is not a pure manufacturing story; the attractive part of the business is the brand, network, and concentrate economics

## 2. Why It Matters

- It is a useful first pitch because the business is familiar, stable, and not dependent on difficult technical forecasting
- The company helps train the right questions for fund management: cash generation, pricing power, margin durability, and capital allocation
- Even if the stock is not an immediate buy, studying it teaches how to evaluate quality businesses that often trade at premium valuations
- The likely debate is not whether the business is good, but whether the current price fairly reflects that quality

## 3. Revenue And Unit Economics

- Main growth drivers: pricing, global volume trends, product mix, and expansion of higher-value categories
- Key margins: gross margin durability, operating margin resilience, and free-cash-flow generation
- Capital intensity: relatively favorable compared with many industrial or manufacturing-heavy businesses
- Important operating metrics: organic revenue growth, pricing versus volume mix, free cash flow, return on invested capital, and brand strength indicators
- Working assumption: this is a business where modest growth plus strong economics can still create acceptable returns if entry valuation is reasonable

## 4. Financial Quality

- Cash conversion: expected to be a major strength and a central reason to study the company
- Balance-sheet strength: should be judged through debt levels, interest burden, and resilience of cash generation
- Accounting concerns: check treatment of restructuring items, brand-related adjustments, and how reported earnings compare with cash generation
- Red flags: weakening volume offset by pricing alone, margin pressure, overreliance on adjustments, or deterioration in return metrics
- Initial view: the business should score well on financial quality, but the real test is whether quality is already fully priced

## 5. Valuation View

- Current multiple or implied expectations: fill this after reviewing the latest filings and market data
- Base case: high-quality, slow-to-moderate growth compounder with durable margins and steady cash generation
- Bull case: pricing power remains durable, category mix improves, and the market continues to pay a premium for stability and brand quality
- Bear case: growth disappoints, consumer preferences shift, input costs pressure margins, and the stock multiple compresses
- Main value drivers: pricing power, volume resilience, margin durability, free-cash-flow conversion, and multiple paid for quality
- Key valuation question: is this a great business at too high a price, or a fair entry into a durable compounder?

## 6. Key Risks

- Business risk: changing consumer preferences away from legacy sugary beverages or slower category growth
- Competitive risk: large beverage competitors and private-label pressure in some channels
- Financial risk: premium valuation can create downside even when business performance remains solid
- Thesis-break conditions: sustained erosion in pricing power, weakening cash conversion, declining return economics, or evidence that brand strength is fading
- Research risk: it is easy to confuse a famous brand with an automatically attractive investment

## 7. Variant Perception Or Edge

- What do I think is mispriced: for now, no strong variant perception yet; this memo is mainly a training case for process quality
- Why might I be right: stable businesses can still be misunderstood when investors overfocus on short-term volume, currency, or sentiment noise
- Why might I be wrong: the market may already understand the business very well and price it correctly as a premium-quality defensive asset
- Current honest view: this is a useful company for learning, but the edge is not yet strong enough to justify high conviction

## 8. Position Conclusion

- Decision: watch
- Level of conviction: low to medium until valuation work is completed
- Suggested position size if owned: small starter size only if valuation becomes clearly attractive
- What would increase conviction: clear evidence of attractive valuation, durable pricing power, healthy cash conversion, and acceptable downside from multiple compression
- Why this is the right current conclusion: the business quality appears attractive, but business quality alone is not enough without a defensible entry price

## 9. Follow-Up Questions

- What does the latest annual report say about pricing versus volume?
- How stable has free-cash-flow conversion been across different environments?
- What valuation range is justified under conservative assumptions?
- How much of the premium multiple is explained by business quality versus investor crowding into safety?
- What would make this become a real buy instead of only a teaching case?

## Next Research Tasks

- Read the latest annual report and summarize the three statements
- Build a simple bear, base, and bull valuation range
- Compare Coca-Cola with one close peer on margins, growth, and cash conversion
- Write a short note on whether the premium valuation is deserved
